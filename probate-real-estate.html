Start with understanding

What is probate real estate?

Probate is the legal process used to administer a person's estate after death. When real property is part of the estate, the person handling the sale must have the legal authority needed to transfer title. Not every inherited home follows the exact same path: a will, the way title was held, the heirs, estate debts and the type of administration can all affect what happens next.

You do not have to empty the house, renovate it or know exactly what paperwork you need before calling me.
I can help you understand the real estate questions and coordinate with the title company and your probate attorney so we can determine what needs to happen before the property is listed or closed.

Independent administration

An independent executor or administrator generally handles estate administration with less ongoing court supervision after appointment, although the representative's actual authority to sell still depends on the will, court orders and Texas law.

Dependent administration

A dependent administrator operates under greater court supervision, and actions involving estate property may require court approval. That can add steps to the real estate timeline.

There is no reliable single “6–18 month” timeline for every Texas inherited-property sale. Some estates move faster and others take considerably longer. I will help plan the real estate timeline around the authority and requirements confirmed by the estate's attorney and title company.

Preparing for a sale

Documents that may come into play

The exact documents needed depend on how the estate and title are being handled. Rather than treating this as a universal checklist, think of these as documents your probate attorney or title company may ask you to locate or obtain.

Death certificate

A certified copy of the deceased property owner's death certificate may be requested as part of the estate and title process.

Will and estate records

If there is a will, keep the original and any probate filings, orders and related estate records available for your attorney and title company.

Proof of authority

Depending on the estate, this may involve Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration, a court order admitting a will as a Muniment of Title, or another legally appropriate method of establishing ownership or authority.

Affidavit of Heirship

In some Texas situations involving real property, an Affidavit of Heirship may be used to establish facts about the decedent's heirs. It does not itself transfer title, and it is not the correct solution for every estate, so the attorney and title company should determine whether it is appropriate.

What might the title company need before closing?

Identity & estate documents

Government-issued identification for the people signing, along with the applicable certified probate orders, letters or other evidence establishing who has authority to convey the property.

Deed & closing documents

The appropriate deed and affidavits will depend on how title is vested and who has authority to sign. The title company and attorney determine the correct instruments for the specific transaction.

Property disclosures

Texas disclosure requirements have exceptions, including certain fiduciary transfers. I will help identify the real estate forms that apply while legal questions are referred to the appropriate attorney.

Mortgages, liens & payoff information

If the property has a mortgage or other liens, the title company may need payoff information and will address title requirements before the sale can close.
