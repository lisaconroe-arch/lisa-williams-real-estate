Should we repair the house before selling?

Not necessarily. We can compare the likely market benefit of repairs with their cost, time and complexity. In some situations, selling as-is may be the more practical approach.

I can provide real estate information about pricing, preparation and offers, but I do not decide legal disputes between spouses. If an issue depends on a court order, settlement agreement or legal rights, that question belongs with your attorneys.

Can you communicate with both sides?

Communication expectations should be established clearly at the beginning and must be consistent with the representation arrangement and any legal requirements. My goal is to keep real estate communication organized, documented and professional.

Do I need to wait until the divorce is final?

Not every situation follows the same timeline. Whether and when a property can or should be sold depends on the owners, agreements, court orders and circumstances. Your attorney should advise you on the legal timing; I can help plan the real estate timeline around it.

Divorce Real Estate Questions & Answers

Selling a home during divorce can raise difficult legal, financial and practical questions. These answers explain the real estate side in plain language. Your Texas family-law attorney should advise you about ownership rights, court orders, temporary arrangements and division of proceeds.

1. Can my spouse sell the house without my permission?

Not necessarily. In Texas, the answer depends on title, whether the home is a marital homestead, applicable community-property rules, and any court orders. A spouse generally cannot simply ignore the other spouse's rights or required signatures. Even if only one spouse appears on the deed, homestead rights and divorce orders may matter. Before anyone signs a listing or sale contract, ask your attorney and the title company to confirm who must consent and sign.

2. Should we sell the house before or after the divorce is final?

Either may be possible. Selling before the divorce is final can reduce ongoing expenses and allow both parties to move forward, but the sale and handling of proceeds may require agreement or court approval. Selling afterward may allow the divorce decree to establish responsibilities first, but it can extend mortgage and upkeep obligations. Your attorneys should determine what is permitted and how proceeds will be held or distributed; Lisa can explain the likely market and sale timeline.

3. How is the equity and money from the sale divided?

Sale proceeds are not the same as equity. The closing statement first accounts for items such as mortgage payoffs, liens, closing costs and agreed compensation. How the remaining funds are divided depends on ownership, any separate-property claims, the parties' agreement and the divorce court's orders. Texas courts generally divide the community estate in a manner that is just and right, which does not necessarily mean 50/50. Ask your attorney to confirm distribution instructions before closing. Estimate potential net proceeds.

4. Who pays the mortgage, taxes, and upkeep while the house is on the market?

That should be addressed in a written agreement or applicable temporary or final court orders. The plan can identify who makes mortgage payments, pays taxes and insurance, handles utilities, lawn care and repairs, and whether anyone receives a credit or reimbursement later. Missing payments can affect credit and the property, even while the divorce is pending. Your REALTOR® can help track property-related tasks but does not decide who is legally responsible.

5. What happens if one of us wants to keep the house instead of selling?

One spouse may propose a buyout or another arrangement, subject to agreement and court orders. Important questions include the home's value, available equity, mortgage qualification, whether refinancing or loan assumption is possible, and how the departing spouse will be released from financial obligations. A deed transfer alone does not remove a borrower from the mortgage. Your attorney and lender should review the options; Lisa can provide market information for the real estate discussion.

6. Who will live in the home and for how long?

Occupancy should be addressed by agreement or court order, particularly if children are involved or a sale is pending. Establish who may stay, the expected move-out date, who maintains the property, how showings will work, and what happens if an offer requires a different timeline. A REALTOR® can coordinate showing notice and access consistent with the listing agreement, but cannot determine possession rights.

7. How will we determine if repairs are needed before putting the home on the market?

Start with a walk-through, known defects, market conditions and the likely buyer pool. Prioritize safety, lender or insurance concerns, and repairs likely to improve the net result. Compare contractor estimates with realistic as-is pricing and the time and carrying costs involved. Both parties should agree on the repair budget and decision process as required by their agreements or court orders. Compare as-is and renovation scenarios.

8. How will we select a REALTOR®?

Look for someone experienced in coordinating sensitive transactions, explaining pricing clearly, communicating neutrally and working appropriately with attorneys and the title company. Ask about the marketing plan, how updates and offers will be shared, showing arrangements, compensation and who the brokerage will represent. Both parties should understand and approve the representation and listing arrangements when required. Lisa can discuss her approach without taking sides in a legal dispute.

9. How will we determine the initial selling price of the home?

Your REALTOR® should prepare a comparative market analysis using recent nearby sales, current competition, condition, location and buyer demand. An appraisal may also be appropriate in some situations. The initial list price should reflect the market and your agreed selling goals, not simply the amount either spouse hopes to receive. Establish in advance how pricing changes will be approved if the property does not attract suitable offers.

10. How will we split the proceeds or liability upon the sale of the home?

Before closing, your attorneys should clarify the written instructions for sale proceeds, debts, credits, reimbursement claims and any shortfall. The title company will calculate the transaction's closing figures, but it should not be asked to decide contested divorce issues. If the sale price is insufficient to cover payoffs and expenses, the parties need a plan for the shortfall and any required lender approval. The divorce agreement or court order—not the REALTOR®—governs the parties' legal division of money and liabilities.
